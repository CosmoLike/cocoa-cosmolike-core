# Covariances of galaxy clustering and weak lensing

This directory computes ingredients for the covariance of galaxy
clustering, galaxy–galaxy lensing and cosmic shear, often called the
**3x2pt** observables. Galaxy clustering measures correlations of galaxy
positions; galaxy–galaxy lensing measures the tangential distortion of
background galaxies around foreground galaxies; cosmic shear correlates
the distortions of two background galaxies.

A model data vector predicts the mean of these measurements. Its
covariance describes their scatter across realizations of the same
cosmology:

$$
\mathcal C_{ij}
= \left\langle
(\hat x_i-\langle\hat x_i\rangle)
(\hat x_j-\langle\hat x_j\rangle)
\right\rangle.
$$

Here a hat denotes a measurement, and each index selects an observable,
a pair of redshift bins and an angular bin or multipole band. Diagonal
entries are variances. Off-diagonal entries describe shared fluctuations:
the same foreground structure can affect several redshift bins and
several observables.

The calculation separates three physical contributions:

- **Gaussian (G):** fluctuations described by products of two power
  spectra, including finite galaxy counts and random intrinsic shapes.
- **Super-sample covariance (SSC):** fluctuations larger than the observed
  region change the clustering measured inside it.
- **Connected non-Gaussian (cNG):** the remaining four-point correlations
  generated by gravitational evolution, beyond Gaussian pairings.

Thus $\mathcal C=\mathcal C^{\rm G}+\mathcal C^{\rm SSC}
+\mathcal C^{\rm cNG}$. The separation lets us inspect the physical origin
of an uncertainty before adding the terms.

**Current scope:** these files provide numerical components. The shared
Python `covariance/survey.py` assembles full real-space G+SSC+cNG forecasts
for massless neutrinos, Limber spectra, linear galaxy bias, zero IA,
magnification and RSD, and a spherical-cap footprint. Independently
validated survey accuracy settings and all-pairs non-Limber corrections
remain open. Covariance integration choices stay separate from the
data-vector calculation.

## From three-dimensional matter to angular spectra

### Radial windows: `spectra_cov.c`

A galaxy catalog occupies a range of distances. A **radial window**
$W_A(\chi)$ describes how much matter at distance $\chi$ contributes to
the projected field $A$. For galaxy density it includes the galaxy
redshift distribution and bias. For lensing it includes the ability of
foreground matter to distort more distant sources.

In a flat universe, a source at $\chi'>\chi$ receives a geometric lensing
weight $1-\chi/\chi'$. Averaging over the normalized source distribution
$n_\chi$ gives the efficiency

$$
g(\chi)=\int_\chi^\infty d\chi'\,
n_\chi(\chi')\left(1-\frac{\chi}{\chi'}\right).
$$

The code separates this into two cumulative integrals:
$g(\chi)=A(\chi)-\chi B(\chi)$. Here $A$ is the fraction of sources behind
the lens, while $B$ is the same fraction weighted by $1/\chi'$. Reusing
these cumulative integrals avoids integrating the entire background
catalog again for every foreground distance.

Multipole $\ell$ labels angular structure: larger $\ell$ means smaller
angular scales. The Limber approximation associates that structure with
a transverse wavenumber $k=(\ell+1/2)/f_K(\chi)$, where $f_K$ is the
transverse comoving distance. It neglects the detailed radial coupling
of the modes, giving

$$
C_\ell^{AB}=
\int d\chi\,
\frac{W_A(\chi,\ell)W_B(\chi,\ell)}{f_K^2(\chi)}
P\!\left(\frac{\ell+1/2}{f_K(\chi)},a(\chi)\right).
$$

$P$ is the three-dimensional power spectrum, and $a=1/(1+z)$ is the
scale factor. The windows can depend on multipole through the field
transfer factors. [Krause & Eifler](https://arxiv.org/abs/1601.05779)
describe the projected fields and their weights in Section 2.1.

The implementation samples all fields on the same radial quadrature,
meaning the same distances and integration weights. It supports linear
galaxy bias, magnification, optional redshift-space distortions and
optional nonlinear alignment (NLA) of source shapes. These are the
implemented field choices; higher-order bias and alignment models require
additional terms. The spectra contain signal only. Noise is added later.

Bias relates galaxy density fluctuations to matter fluctuations.
Magnification changes the observed counts through lensing; peculiar
velocities cause redshift-space distortions by changing observed
redshifts. Intrinsic alignment describes coherent galaxy shapes caused
by their environment, in addition to the distortion caused by lensing.

### Why covariance needs additional cross spectra: `gaussian_cov.c`

Suppose we measure spectra $AB$ and $CD$. Their covariance involves four
fields, even though each measurement involves only two. For Gaussian
fields, subtracting the product of the means removes the pairing
$(AB)(CD)$. The two remaining pairings are $(AC)(BD)$ and $(AD)(BC)$.

Writing $\widetilde C^{AB}=C^{AB}+N^{AB}$ for signal plus noise, the
integer-multipole approximation used by `gaussian_wick_cov` is

$$
\mathcal C^{\rm G}_{AB,CD}(\ell,\ell')
=
\frac{\delta_{\ell\ell'}}{(2\ell+1)f_{\rm sky}}
\left[
\widetilde C_\ell^{AC}\widetilde C_\ell^{BD}
+\widetilde C_\ell^{AD}\widetilde C_\ell^{BC}
\right].
$$

There are $2\ell+1$ full-sky modes at each $\ell$;
$f_{\rm sky}=\Omega_s/(4\pi)$ approximates the reduction from a finite
survey area $\Omega_s$. The Kronecker delta makes different multipoles
uncorrelated in this approximation. This is the single-multipole form of
the Gaussian expression in
[Krause & Eifler, Appendix A](https://arxiv.org/abs/1601.05779).
An arbitrary footprint also couples multipoles; the area approximation
does not include that full coupling.

Let $g_i$ denote galaxy density in lens bin $i$, and $\gamma_j$ shear
in source bin $j$. The covariance between $g_i\gamma_j$ and $g_k\gamma_l$
needs $g_i g_k$, $\gamma_j\gamma_l$, $g_i\gamma_l$ and
$\gamma_j g_k$. Excluding $g_i g_k$ from the measured data vector does
not remove it from this equation. This is why `spectra_cov.c` computes
every lens/source pair, including pairs absent from the data vector.

## Galaxy counts, shape noise and the footprint

### Analytic noise: `gaussian_cov.c`

For independent catalogs with angular number density $\bar n$ per
steradian, the white-noise powers are

$$
N_g=\frac{1}{\bar n_g},
\qquad
N_\gamma=\frac{\sigma_{\rm component}^2}{\bar n_s}.
$$

The shape dispersion is for **one ellipticity component**. White noise
means its power is independent of multipole. Catalogs may overlap in
redshift without sharing objects; these noise formulas assume independent
objects across distinct catalogs, with zero noise cross power.

Expanding the Gaussian products gives signal–signal, signal–noise and
noise–noise terms. In real space, pure white noise corresponds to
coincident pairs. A truncated multipole sum spreads that contribution
over nearby separations instead of representing it exactly.
`gaussian_noise_pair_cov` therefore evaluates pure noise from pair counts,
while the projected harmonic part retains the other terms. For harmonic
band powers, `gaussian_wick_cov` can retain the full noise–noise term.

### Pair area: `mask_cov.c`

Let $W(\hat n)$ be one inside the observed footprint and zero outside.
For an angular bin $b$, the ordered-pair area is

$$
A_{{\rm pair},b}
=\int d\Omega_1\,d\Omega_2\,
W(\hat n_1)W(\hat n_2)\,
\mathbf{1}_b(\theta_{12}).
$$

The indicator $\mathbf{1}_b$ selects pairs whose separation lies in the bin.
Multiplying this area by two catalog densities gives their expected
ordered-pair count. Survey edges remove possible partners, reducing the
count and increasing the noise. For matching angular bins, the pure-noise
covariance is proportional to $N_A N_B/A_{{\rm pair},b}$.
The code's estimator-dependent factors account for repeated catalog
pairings and the two shear components.

`annulus_pair_area_cov` gives the boundary-free approximation
$\Omega_s\,2\pi(\cos\theta_{\rm low}-\cos\theta_{\rm high})$.
`mask_pair_area_cov` instead obtains the area from the mask's angular
power spectrum. It assumes a common binary footprint and uniform noise;
distinct footprints or object weights need their corresponding pair
counts. [Friedrich et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.08568), Section 4
and Appendix C, explain the noise and survey-pair geometry.

The mask spectrum is **raw**, with
$C_L^W=\sum_m|w_{Lm}|^2/(2L+1)$ and
$C_0^W=\Omega_s^2/(4\pi)$; $w_{Lm}$ are its spherical-harmonic
coefficients. The same normalization is used by `ssc_cov.c`.
Mask multipoles describe the footprint, so their monopole and dipole
are retained even when an estimator removes those modes from the
cosmological signal.

## Nonlinear structure: the connected four-point function

### Halo mass integrals: `halo_cov.c`

In the halo model, matter is grouped into collapsed objects with
normalized Fourier profiles $u(k|M,a)$, where $u(0|M,a)=1$.
A correlation involving several density factors from the same halo
contains one profile for each factor. The module collects these into

$$
I_\mu^\beta(k_1,\ldots,k_\mu;a)
=\int d\ln M\,
\frac{dn}{d\ln M}\,
b_\beta(M,a)
\left(\frac{M}{\bar\rho_{cb}}\right)^\mu
\prod_{r=1}^{\mu}u(k_r|M,a).
$$

Here $dn/d\ln M$ counts halos per comoving volume and logarithmic mass
interval. The exponent $\mu$ counts density factors. The bias weight is
$b_0=1$ or $b_1$, the linear response of halo abundance to the surrounding
density. [Cooray & Sheth](https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/0206508)
review the halo-model construction.

Here $\bar\rho_{cb}$ is the mean comoving density of cold dark matter
plus baryons. The implementation uses this cb field for halo abundance,
variance and the moment's density normalization. It
reuses the core concentration and Navarro–Frenk–White (NFW) profile.
The core $M_{200m}$ convention defines a radius enclosing 200 times
the total mean matter density. These cb moments alone are **not** a
complete total-matter trispectrum with massive neutrinos.

The mass quadrature is shared by all moments. Abundance, bias,
concentration and profiles are reused across wavenumber pairs.
Only $I_1^1$ receives an unresolved-low-mass completion, chosen so that
$I_1^1(0)=1$ on that quadrature. The higher moments require convergence
with the mass range and integration resolution.

### Gravitational mode coupling: `perturbation_cov.c`

Gravitational evolution couples initially independent density modes.
At leading order, perturbation theory predicts their bispectrum
(three-point function) and connected trispectrum (four-point function).
These provide correlations between different halos in the assembly below.
The perturbative kernels are described by
[Bernardeau et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/0112551).

`tree_averages_cov` averages the required power, bispectrum and
trispectrum over the angle between two transverse wavevectors of lengths
$K$ and $Q$. Because the projected wavevectors lie in a plane, the
average uses $d\theta/\pi$ for $0<\theta<\pi$, rather than the
three-dimensional orientation measure $d\cos\theta/2$.

For the covariance configuration
$(\boldsymbol k,-\boldsymbol k,\boldsymbol q,-\boldsymbol q)$,
some internal wavevectors approach zero. The implementation combines
canceling terms before evaluation near this limit. Exactly zero internal
momentum channels assigned to SSC are excluded from the cNG average.

### Combining halo contributions: `non_gaussian_cov.c`

Four density factors can belong to one, two, three or four halos.
Two halos allow two distinct allocations: one factor in one halo and
three in the other, or two in each. `halo_trispectrum_cov` returns all
five terms separately:

$$
\overline T=
\overline T_{1h}
+\overline T_{2h,13}
+\overline T_{2h,22}
+\overline T_{3h}
+\overline T_{4h}.
$$

For example, the one-halo term is $I_4^0(K,K,Q,Q)$.
The other terms combine halo moments with the angular averages above.
This organization follows the halo partitions in
[Takada & Hu, Section III](https://arxiv.org/abs/1302.6994).

For fields represented by radial windows, the corresponding Limber
projection has the form

$$
\mathcal C^{\rm cNG}_{AB,CD}(\ell,\ell')
=\frac{1}{\Omega_s}\int d\chi\,
\frac{W_AW_BW_CW_D}{f_K^6}\,
\overline T\!\left(
\frac{\ell+1/2}{f_K},\frac{\ell'+1/2}{f_K};a
\right).
$$

All windows are evaluated at the same distance, with their appropriate
multipoles. The windows and trispectrum must refer to the same density
field. This equation explains how the returned three-dimensional
trispectrum enters a survey calculation. The assembler itself adds no
survey area, radial weights, galaxy shot-noise trispectrum or angular
binning. Those require a consistent survey-level model.

## Super-sample covariance: `ssc_cov.c`

A survey can lie in a region whose mean density is slightly above or
below the cosmic mean. That background fluctuation changes the abundance
of halos and the growth and apparent scale of smaller structures.
Several measured spectra therefore shift together, even when the
background mode is too large to measure within the footprint.

The power response $D=\partial P/\partial\delta_b$ quantifies this effect:
a small background overdensity $\delta_b$ changes the power by
$D\,\delta_b$ to first order. The response approach is developed by
[Takada & Hu](https://arxiv.org/abs/1302.6994).

`halo_response_cov` in `non_gaussian_cov.c` supplies a halo-model response.
It combines the one-halo abundance response with the growth and dilation
of the two-halo power; dilation means that a fixed observed scale samples
a shifted physical wavenumber. Its coefficients and power-spectrum slope
are explicit inputs. Transferring the halo model's fractional response
to another supplied power spectrum is an optional modeling choice,
not an algebraic identity.

### From a matter response to a measured-spectrum response

`ssc_shell_response_cov` computes the contribution from each radial shell.
Define $\Phi_{AB}$ through
$\delta C_\ell^{AB}=\int d\chi\,\Phi_{AB}(\ell,\chi)\delta_b(\chi)$.
The implemented expression is

$$
\Phi_{AB}(\ell,\chi)
=\frac{W_AW_B}{f_K^2}D(k,\chi)
-\left[U_A(\chi)+U_B(\chi)\right]C_\ell^{AB}.
$$

The first term projects the change in matter clustering. The second
accounts for how the measurement defines galaxy density: dividing counts
by their **survey mean** removes part of the background fluctuation.
Here $U_A$ is defined by
$\delta\bar n_A/\bar n_A=\int d\chi\,U_A(\chi)\delta_b(\chi)$.
Because this normalization acts on the whole projected field, its
correction multiplies the whole spectrum $C_\ell^{AB}$.
A field with no such mean normalization has $U_A=0$.

These mean-response windows must describe the actual estimator.
They cannot generally be replaced by its short-scale clustering windows.

### How the survey footprint weights the background

`ssc_mask_variance_cov` uses the raw mask spectrum and linear matter
power to calculate the strength of the survey-averaged background at
each distance:

$$
s_b(\chi)=\frac{1}{\Omega_s^2 f_K^2(\chi)}
\sum_L (2L+1)C_L^W
P_{\rm lin}\!\left(\frac{L+1/2}{f_K(\chi)},a(\chi)\right).
$$

The footprint weights each background angular mode through $C_L^W$;
the area squared normalizes two survey averages. This calculation uses
the **long-mode Limber approximation**, replacing the correlation
between different radial shells by

$$
\langle\delta_b(\chi)\delta_b(\chi')\rangle
=\delta_{\rm D}(\chi-\chi')\,s_b(\chi).
$$

The returned quantity is named `sigma2` in the interface; here it is
written as $s_b$ to emphasize that it has units of length. It is not the
dimensionless variance of a finite-width shell: the Dirac delta carries
inverse-length units.

Under this approximation, two observable responses give

$$
\mathcal C^{\rm SSC}_{ij}
=\int d\chi\,s_b(\chi)\Phi_i(\chi)\Phi_j(\chi).
$$

Each shell thus contributes a weighted outer product of the same
response vector with itself. Using common responses and positive
integration weights preserves the positive-semidefinite structure.

To see why, consider any weighted sum of measurements with coefficients
$v_i$. Its SSC variance is

$$
v^{\mathsf T}\mathcal C^{\rm SSC}v
=\int d\chi\,s_b(\chi)
\left[\sum_i v_i\Phi_i(\chi)\right]^2\ge0.
$$

Different lens bins can respond to the same background fluctuation.
Their cross terms are part of this square, including when their mutual
clustering spectrum is absent from the data vector. Deleting selected
cross-lens terms while retaining their correlations with shear can break
this structure and give a negative variance. Interpolate the common
responses before forming their products, and retain every requested
cross block when assembling separately computed pieces of the matrix.

The long-mode Limber approximation needs its own accuracy assessment;
full-sky angular transforms do not make it exact. Correlations between
distinct radial shells and a complete nonlinear tidal response require
additional treatment. Tides describe direction-dependent stretching,
beyond the scalar density change used above.
[Barreira, Krause & Schmidt](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.07467)
derive density and tidal SSC, including curved-sky predictions beyond
Limber for the long modes.

## From spectra to measured bins: `operators_cov.c`

Measurements average over finite angular intervals. For an angular bin
$b$, the code constructs an operator

$$
O_{b\ell}
=\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}\,
\frac{\int_{\theta_{\rm low}}^{\theta_{\rm high}}
d\theta\,\sin\theta\,d_\ell(\theta)}
{\cos\theta_{\rm low}-\cos\theta_{\rm high}}.
$$

The $\sin\theta$ factor is the spherical annulus measure.
The angular function $d_\ell$ depends on the observable: galaxy density
uses a scalar Legendre polynomial, while shear requires spin-two
rotation functions because its components depend on the orientation of
the pair. In a basis aligned with the pair, shear has tangential and
cross components. $\xi_+$ sums their two correlations; $\xi_-$ takes
their difference. The other operators give tangential shear around
galaxies, $\gamma_t$, and galaxy clustering, $w(\theta)$.
`bandpower_operator_cov` instead weights each integer multipole within
a Fourier band by its normalized $2\ell+1$ mode count.

If $x_b=\sum_\ell O_{b\ell}C_\ell$, the covariance transforms on **both**
indices:

$$
\mathcal C^{\,x}
=O_{\rm left}\,\mathcal C^{\,C}\,O_{\rm right}^{\mathsf T}.
$$

Each side corresponds to one of the two measurements whose covariance
we seek. Transforming only one side leaves the other in harmonic space.
`gaussian_project_cov` performs this contraction for a diagonal harmonic
Gaussian covariance. A general SSC or cNG matrix also couples distinct
multipoles. The bin-averaging and spin conventions are discussed by
[Friedrich et al., Appendices A and B](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.08568).

The operators expect observed-shear spectra. The current core harmonic
convention needs a factor
$\sqrt{(\ell-1)(\ell+2)/[\ell(\ell+1)]}$ per source leg at $\ell\ge2$
before using these operators. That conversion belongs to the signal;
white shape noise remains $\sigma_{\rm component}^2/\bar n_s$.
Spin-two rows have no $\ell<2$ contribution. Scalar rows retain the
monopole and dipole; any estimator-specific removal must be consistent.

The operator's angular quadrature and maximum multipole must be refined
together. Evaluating a bin at its center does not perform the integral
above.

## Units, numerical structure and remaining scope

Core radial distances use $c/H_0$, and wavenumbers use its inverse.
Power spectra, bispectra and trispectra consequently carry the third,
sixth and ninth powers of that length unit. Radial windows have inverse
length units. Angles are in radians, areas in steradians, pair areas in
steradians squared, and catalog densities per steradian. Halo masses
use $M_\odot/h$. Supplied arrays must follow the same conventions.

The implementation shares geometry, profiles and quadrature inputs
across related outputs. OpenMP assigns independent work to different
threads. SIMDe lets one processor instruction apply the same arithmetic
to several numbers, called vector lanes; the source comments identify
what each lane represents. These choices accelerate the stated equations
without selecting the physical approximation or its integration accuracy.

The shared survey assembly retains all cross-bin SSC/cNG blocks and uses
common observed-field conventions for every probe. Its mask model is a
spherical cap. All-pairs non-Limber cross spectra and a calibrated
massive-neutrino model remain open. The halo response choices and finite
integration grids do not constitute a validated survey accuracy setting.

A usable covariance must give nonnegative variance to every linear
combination of measurements: $v^{\mathsf T}\mathcal C v\ge0$.
The selected matrix must be positive definite if it is to be inverted.
An individual cross entry or a separated approximate cNG contribution
need not be positive. Negative modes in the total covariance require
investigation of the model, pair coverage and numerical convergence;
clipping eigenvalues does not validate the calculation.

Numerical refinement must also stabilize the inferred parameter errors
and Figure of Merit, commonly proportional to the inverse area of a
two-parameter confidence region. Small entrywise differences alone do
not establish that. No universal production integration setting or
complete survey runtime is asserted by these component interfaces.

## Notebook calculations

The [shared Python tools](../../cosmolike_notebook_utils/covariance/README.md)
prepare survey-independent inputs, build requested Gaussian blocks and
inspect covariance eigenvalues. Project adapters supply redshift files,
cosmology and number densities. The LSST Y1 project contains the executable
`EXAMPLE_EVALUATE_COVARIANCE.ipynb` and a `covariance/` input adapter.
The example computes a single-source Gaussian shear covariance; it is not
a full G+SSC+cNG survey generator.

[Shared Matplotlib functions](../../cosmolike_notebook_utils/plot_covariances.py)
show split-triangle correlation comparisons, component maps and histograms,
and angular standard-deviation panels. They receive arrays only and never
alter the covariance to make a plot appear positive or well behaved.
